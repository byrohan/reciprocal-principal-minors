Reciprocal principal minors and the limits of diagonal similarity

Abstract. An irreducible real matrix can have the same corresponding principal minors as its inverse without being diagonally similar to the inverse, even after transposition. The smallest possible order is five. We give an integer witness with entries of magnitude at most two, an infinite rational construction and dense examples with positive principal minors. Theorem 7 of Schlecht and Habets (IEEE, 2017) already fails in order four. We locate the failure of the proposed induction and give a separate proof of the characterization by diagonal similarity to a unitary matrix for networks that are lossless for every choice of delays.

1. A five-dimensional disproof

Let M[S] denote the principal submatrix indexed by S, with empty determinant one. Write M ∼ N if N = D−1MD for an invertible diagonal D. Irreducibility means that the directed graph with arcs i → j whenever Mij ≠ 0 is strongly connected. This is equivalent to the absence of a proper block triangular form under simultaneous permutation.

2. A Cayley-transform criterion

For I ± H invertible, write C(H) = (I + H)(I − H)−1. Its inverse is C(−H). The change of coordinates used here and its inverse H = (C − I)(C + I)−1 commute with transposition and diagonal similarity. Rational functions preserve block triangular form whenever their denominators are invertible. Thus C(H) is irreducible exactly when H is.

Lemma 2. The corresponding principal minors of C(H) and C(−H) agree precisely when every principal minor of H of odd order vanishes. More generally, matrices with equal corresponding principal minors retain this equality under the same admissible Cayley transform.

Proof. For diagonal Z = diag(z1,…,zn), the coefficient of ∏j∈S zj in FM(Z) = det(I + ZM) is det M[S], by row multilinearity. Multiplication inside the determinant gives

det(I − H) FC(H)(Z) = det(I + Z + (Z − I)H).

At the sign vector having −1 precisely on S, the right side equals 2n(−1)|S| det H[S]. Indeed, outside S the rows select the identity, while on S they select −H. Two multiaffine polynomials that agree on every sign vector agree identically, by interpolation in each coordinate.

If FC(H) = FC(−H), first put S = ∅ to obtain equal denominators. The evaluations then force each odd minor to equal its negative. Conversely, vanishing odd minors give equal denominators by expanding det(I ± H) as sums of principal minors. All sign-vector evaluations agree. The same calculation applies to two matrices having equal corresponding minors, including their equal denominators. ■

Removing the exceptional eigenvalue

Suppose a real inverse pair has equal corresponding minors. Choose a diagonal sign matrix J with det(I + JA) ≠ 0. Such a choice exists because the average of det(I + JA) over all signs is its constant coefficient, 1. Put M = JA. The corresponding minors of M and M−1 = A−1J retain equality, since each acquires the same product of signs. Row signs preserve support.

For a diagonal D, the equation D−1MD = M−1 gives (DJ)−1A(DJ) = A−1. The transpose equation gives D−1ATD = A−1 directly. We can therefore use H = (M − I)(M + I)−1 to prove a lower bound even when −1 is an eigenvalue of the original matrix.

A small-order fact for general pairs

For irreducible matrices of order at most three, corresponding principal minors determine diagonal similarity up to transposition [4]. They fix all loop weights and two-cycle products. In order three the determinant then determines the sum of the two oriented triangle products, and the product of those two numbers is the product of the three two-cycle products. Their unordered pair is therefore fixed. Transpose one matrix if necessary to align the triangle products.

Now every simple directed cycle has the same product in both matrices. Each nonzero edge in a strongly connected support lies on a nonzero simple cycle, so the two supports agree. The ratios of corresponding nonzero entries multiply to one around every closed walk, by decomposition into cycles. Propagating a nonzero diagonal entry along directed paths from a root yields consistent values: append a common return path to compare any two paths. This constructs the diagonal similarity over the original field.

3. Why order five is minimal over ℝ

Theorem 3. For every invertible irreducible real matrix of order at most four, equality of the corresponding principal minors with its inverse implies one of the two diagonal similarities in the conjecture.

Proof. Apply the sign change of §2. Lemma 2 gives an irreducible real H with vanishing odd principal minors. Its diagonal is zero. It suffices to prove H ∼ −H or HT ∼ −H, since the Cayley transform carries these alternatives back to M and M−1.

If the directed support has no triangle, every simple directed cycle has even length: loops are absent and there are at most four vertices. Every closed directed walk therefore has even length. Fix a root. All directed paths from the root to a given vertex have the same parity, as one sees by appending a fixed return path. Every arc joins opposite parities. The diagonal signs of the two parity classes conjugate H to −H.

Otherwise fix a directed triangle. Its zero determinant says its forward and reverse cycle products sum to zero. All its reverse arcs are therefore present. For each bidirected edge prescribe the ratio

dj / di = rij = −hji / hij.

On the triangle the product of the three ratios is one by the determinant identity. Choose any nonzero d at one vertex and propagate it around the triangle.

If there is a fourth vertex v, it has at least one incoming and one outgoing arc to the triangle by strong connectivity. For an incoming arc i → v, choose an outgoing v → j. If j = i the arc is already bidirected. Otherwise i → v → j → i is a triangle, so its reverse exists. The same argument with all arrows reversed treats every outgoing arc. Thus every neighbor of v is bidirected. Any two neighbors form a triangle with v; the unit product of its ratios shows that either neighbor gives the same value for dv. A single neighbor gives one unconstrained choice of ratio. The resulting nonzero diagonal satisfies D−1HD = −HT on every entry. Transposing gives the required alternative. This also covers order three when there is no fourth vertex. Theorem 1 attains the bound. ■

Which rank condition repairs the conjecture?

Loewy [5] proves the conclusion for any principal-minor-equivalent partner if, for every partition into two parts of size at least two, at least one of the two cross-blocks has rank at least two. The order-five example has both cross-ranks one across {0,1} and its complement. The condition suffices for the real conjecture and for the complex IEEE theorem (apply it to the entrywise conjugate). An orthogonal example can violate it and still satisfy the transpose alternative [1]. It therefore repairs the general implication while allowing individual inverse pairs to satisfy the conclusion under weaker assumptions.

The two six-dimensional examples

Construct a second zero-diagonal generator G from a triangle and a square sharing only 0. Give 0 → 1 → 2 → 0 weight +1 and its reverse weight −1. Give 0 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 0 weight +1 in the forward direction only. All other entries vanish. An odd-order nonzero determinant term contains an odd cycle, necessarily the triangle. Reversing it pairs terms of opposite weight and the same permutation sign. Thus every odd principal minor vanishes.

As before, the triangle obstructs diagonal similarity to −G and the square obstructs the transpose alternative. Strong connectivity proves irreducibility. The common denominator is 1 + 3t2 − t4 − t6. At t = 1, C(G) is integral, has determinant one and trace ten. Against its inverse the entry (0,1) changes from 2 to 0; the products on 0 → 3 → 4 → 0 for its transpose and inverse are 8 and 16. Diagonal factors cancel around a cycle.

At t = ½ the matrix and inverse have common denominator 107 and full support. Their distinct principal minors are {43,45,51,53,107,117}/107. A real matrix with strictly positive principal minors is a P-matrix. Thus the conjecture fails even for dense P-matrices, where support alone cannot distinguish the alternatives. Full entries and the two 64-row exact comparisons accompany the paper. Both examples also follow from the family proof.

5. An order-four complex obstruction

In Schlecht and Habets [2], A∗ denotes entrywise conjugation and AH the conjugate transpose. We use a bar for entrywise conjugation. Their Theorem 7 [2, p. 1558] assumes only invertibility, irreducibility and equality of the principal minors of A and A−1. It asserts that one of A and AH is diagonally similar to A−1. The real specialization is the 2016 question [1], and the two support obstructions in Theorem 1 also exclude complex diagonal scalings.

Proof. Direct expansion gives det(I − iK) = 6 + 2i and det(I + iK) = 6 − 2i. The cross-block entries between {0,1} and {2,3} are all one, so the support is strongly connected. This gives invertibility and irreducibility of A.

The conjugate of K is obtained by interchanging vertices 0 and 1. The two vertices have the same diagonal and identical incoming and outgoing entries to the other two. As in §4, every corresponding principal minor of K and its conjugate agrees. Common scalar multiplication preserves equality by order. By Lemma 2, A = C(−iK) and A−1 = C(−iK) have equal corresponding minors.

Diagonal similarity of either proposed pair would give K ∼ K or KH ∼ K by the inverse Cayley transform. The four cross-block ones force all four diagonal multipliers to be equal. A scalar conjugation fixes each entry. Yet entry (0,1) distinguishes K from its conjugate, and entry (2,3) distinguishes K from its adjoint. For smaller orders, A and A−1 are irreducible: conjugation preserves support, and a block triangular inverse would give a block triangular original. The small-order argument in §2 therefore applies. ■

The complex base case in Appendix B

The published proof [2, p. 1563] treats order four by invoking Jacobi’s identity in (71)–(72). Its use for nonprincipal minors requires care. For B = D−1(A)−1D, a mixed minor on row set U and column set V acquires the factor ∏v∈Vdv/∏u∈Udu. The complementary minor has rows Vc, columns Uc, the Jacobi sign, conjugation, and denominator det A. The unweighted equalities used there do not follow from diagonal similarity and Jacobi’s identity as stated. For an explicit check in their normal form, take A = C(iK/2) and D = diag(3,⅓,1,1). The matrices A,B just defined have forms (69)–(70) with f2 = 9, f3 = 3 + 3i/2 and e4 = (12 − 6i)/5. In the paper’s one-based indices, the two sides of (71) are (16 − 20i)/41 and (162 − 18i)/41. The product asserted to vanish in (73) is (−1172 − 52i)/41. The real base case admits the independent proof in §3.

6. The induction and its consequences

Appendix B of [2, p. 1562] defines S1 as the indices whose deletion leaves a diagonally similar pair and S2 as those that qualify after transposing one matrix. Its induction asserts that for n ≥ 5 their union has at least five members. For a real inverse pair the smaller pair must itself meet the inverse hypothesis for that induction to apply.

Let R be the complement of a single index j and B = A−1. Matrix multiplication gives the exact identity

A[R]B[R] = IR − A[R,j] B[j,R].

The correction term need not vanish. In the order-six integer example of §4, deleting 3 gives (0,0) entry 5 instead of 1. Irreducibility can also fail after deletion. In that example deleting 1 or 2 makes the surviving matrix reducible. Equality of smaller principal minors survives, but by itself it is the hypothesis of a different problem.

The numerical premise of the gluing argument fails even for the order-five example in Theorem 1. If an index in {2,3,4} is deleted, the direct support obstruction at (0,1) survives. In particular, deletion of 2 retains both the direct obstruction and the transpose obstruction (3,4). Thus at most four of the five deletions qualify, already contradicting the required lower bound. Exact ratio propagation actually finds no qualifying deletion of the five. This identifies a failed implication independently of the complex base-case error.

Consequences for the published results

The dichotomy after Lemma 5 for all irreducible matrices with self-inversive principal minors is false. The proof of Theorem 8 invokes Theorem 7 at its first step. The necessity direction in Theorem 1 invokes Theorem 8. Figure 3, the appeal to Theorem 7 in §III-E and the claim of a complete characterization in §V use this chain. Losslessness for one fixed delay tuple is also insufficient to extract every minor separately from a polynomial with coincident exponents.

A second distinction matters in reading the literal Theorem 1. Its alternative (2) allows any nonsingular diagonal E with AEAH = E. Duchamp’s 2015 example [6], with diagonal entries √2 and both off-diagonals 1, preserves diag(1,−1). Its eigenvalues are √2 ± 1, so it fails the equal-delay test. This also contradicts the unrestricted unitary conclusion in the second branch of Lemma 5. The journal’s own example (6) preserves diag(1,−2) and fails its test (10). A positive definite diagonal metric is equivalent to diagonal similarity to a unitary matrix. Section 7 proves that this repaired criterion characterizes universal losslessness.

7. Recovering the unitary characterization

Theorem 5. Let A be an irreducible complex matrix. A is diagonally similar to a unitary matrix if and only if, for every tuple m of positive integer delays, all zeros of det(diag(zmj) − A) lie on the unit circle. This proof uses the stable determinantal representation theorem of Al Ahmadieh and Vinzant [7].

Proof. Write p(w) = det(diag(w) − A). Assume the delay condition. Taking all delays one yields |det A| = 1. We first prove p(w) ≠ 0 whenever all |wj| < 1. Fix such a point and fix θ with θ/(2π) irrational. For wj ≠ 0 choose positive integers mj,k with

mj,k/k → −log |wj|,   exp(iθmj,k) → wj/|wj|.

To see existence, start at the integer part of −k log |wj| and take the closest phase among the next ⌊√k⌋ iterates. The covering radius of these iterates tends to zero by density of an irrational rotation, uniformly in the starting phase. For a zero coordinate take mj,k = k2. On Re s > 0 the functions p((exp(mj,k(iθ − s/k)))j) are zero-free. They converge locally uniformly to the evaluation of p at coordinates (wj/|wj|)exp(s log |wj|), with zero coordinates fixed at zero. The limit tends to p(0) ≠ 0 along the positive real axis. Hurwitz’s theorem makes the limit zero-free; setting s = 1 proves the assertion.

The polynomial p is self-reciprocal up to a unit scalar: p(w) = c(∏ wj)conj(p(1/w)), with componentwise reciprocals. Indeed, delays 1,2,4,… give distinct subset exponents, and a monic polynomial with all roots on the circle equals a unit multiple of its conjugate reciprocal. Comparing coefficients proves the multivariate identity. Rotating A by a common unit scalar preserves this identity (with possibly different c) and polydisk nonvanishing. Choose the rotation so that 1 is not an eigenvalue and denote the rotated matrix again by A.

Put L = i(I + A)(I − A)−1 and φ(x) = (x − i)/(x + i). Direct row multiplication gives

det(diag(x) − L) = ∏(xj + i) · p(φ(xj)) / p(1,…,1).

The right side is zero-free when all Im xj > 0. For real x its numerator equals c times its conjugate by self-reciprocity, as does the denominator. Thus the normalized polynomial has real coefficients. The matrix L is irreducible since the inverse rational transformation preserves block triangularity. Its determinantal polynomial is irreducible by [8, preprint Lemma 5.1]. Theorem 6.6 of [7] now makes L diagonally similar to a Hermitian matrix. Its inverse transform (L − iI)(L + iI)−1 is therefore diagonally similar to a unitary matrix, and undoing the rotation preserves this property.

Conversely, diagonal similarity leaves every delay determinant unchanged. For unitary U, a root requires Uv = diag(zmj)v for some nonzero v. The two norms differ strictly whenever |z| ≠ 1. For reducible matrices the determinant factors over the strongly connected diagonal blocks, giving the componentwise criterion. ■

8. Provenance and verification

The April 2016 question [1] asks for the real inverse-pair implication without a cut-rank hypothesis. The June 2016 preprint [2, p. 5] and the published Theorem 7 [2, p. 1558] state the same complex implication. The published Appendix B [2, pp. 1562–1563] retains the deletion induction and equations (69)–(73). The rank assumption is expressly dropped on p. 1562. Schlecht’s dissertation [3, p. 57, Theorem 10 and Appendix 4.B] repeats the claim. The journal adds §IV-D on design by unitary mixing, diagonal rescaling and block assembly. Those constructions retain their independent justifications.

General pairs with identical principal minors have been studied since before [4]. Rank-one cuts and cut-transpose equivalence [5,8,9] explain why a single global diagonal similarity can fail. The additional inverse constraint, the sharp real threshold and the positive-minor witnesses give the present question its content. The checked public records yielded no earlier disproof of the precise inverse-pair assertion. This is a search finding, subject to incomplete indexing.

The accompanying Lean 4 disproof of the real statement uses the original order-six witness. A separate kernel proof verifies the five-dimensional witness under the complex hypotheses. Another checks Gaussian-integer certificates for the four-dimensional example. Independent rational and Gaussian-integer computations check the displayed examples. The dimension bounds and the stable-polynomial argument are ordinary proofs. OpenAI Codex developed the construction, proofs, code and manuscript under human direction. Independent peer review is pending.
